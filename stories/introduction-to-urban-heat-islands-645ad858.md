## Cities Under Pressure: The Physics of Urban Heat
As global average temperatures rise, urban centers experience amplified warming due to the **Urban Heat Island (UHI)** effect. Densely built urban fabrics—characterized by impermeable asphalt, dark roofing materials, steel, and a relative lack of canopy cover—absorb and retain vast amounts of incoming solar radiation during daylight hours. At night, while surrounding rural landscapes cool rapidly through radiative heat loss and uninhibited evapotranspiration, artificial surfaces gradually release stored thermal energy back into the boundary layer, preventing ambient cooling.

Spaceborne thermal infrared sensors, notably aboard the [Copernicus Sentinel-3](https://sentinels.copernicus.eu/web/sentinel/missions/sentinel-3) and [NASA/USGS Landsat](https://landsat.gsfc.nasa.gov/) missions, measure **Land Surface Temperature (LST)** with high precision. By combining thermal observations with vegetation indicators like the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) from [Copernicus Sentinel-2](https://sentinels.copernicus.eu/web/sentinel/missions/sentinel-2), researchers quantify microclimatic discrepancies between tree-lined parks and contiguous asphalt corridors. These surface temperature anomalies frequently exceed 10°C to 15°C across European and global metropolitan cores during prolonged heatwaves, amplifying public health risks, air pollution stagnation, and municipal energy demands.

## Earth Observation for Climate-Resilient Urban Design
Mitigating the urban heat island effect requires integrating satellite radiometric observations into municipal master plans. Interventions including high-albedo cool roofs, permeable bioswales, expanded street tree canopies, and preserved ecological ventilation corridors have demonstrated local surface cooling between 2°C and 8°C. Real-time satellite surveillance helps civil protection agencies establish early warning protocols, pinpointing vulnerable neighborhoods requiring targeted heat resilience infrastructure.
