## 1. What is El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO)?

The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is an irregularly periodic variation in winds and sea surface temperatures over the tropical eastern Pacific Ocean. Under normal conditions, trade winds blow steadily westward across the tropical Pacific, pushing sun-warmed surface water toward Indonesia and the western Pacific, while pulling cool, nutrient-rich water up along the coast of South America—a process known as upwelling.

During an El Niño event, these trade winds weaken or even reverse. As a result, the warm surface water sloshes eastward toward the Americas, capping the cold upwelling along the equator and the South American coastline.

## 3. The Atmospheric Teleconnection: How the Tropics Talk to the Poles

When warm water shifts to the central and eastern Pacific, the primary atmospheric convective zone follows. Torrential rainfall and towering thunderstorms migrate from Southeast Asia into the central Pacific basin.

This shift perturbs the subtropical jet streams, acting like a giant boulder dropped into a swift river. The ripples alter the trajectory of mid-latitude storm tracks, creating planetary waves—known as teleconnections—that propagate atmospheric anomalies to North America, Africa, Europe, and beyond.

## 5. Marine Ecosystems: Thermal Stress and Coral Bleaching

Coral reefs are exceptionally sensitive to ambient water temperature variations. When elevated temperatures persist for multiple weeks, corals experience physiological stress, expelling the symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae) living inside their tissues. This turns corals stark white in an event known as coral bleaching.

Prolonged thermal anomalies during intense El Niño years—such as 1997–98 and 2015–16—have triggered global mass bleaching events, devastating sensitive ecosystems in the Great Barrier Reef, the Galápagos, and the Caribbean.

## 6. Monitoring the Cycle: Earth Observation and the Oceanic Niño Index

Scientists track the evolution of ENSO using the Oceanic Niño Index (ONI), which computes a three-month running mean of sea surface temperature anomalies in the east-central tropical Pacific (the Niño 3.4 region between 5°N–5°S, 120°W–170°W).

When anomalies exceed +0.5°C for five consecutive overlapping periods alongside characteristic atmospheric responses, an El Niño is officially declared. Satellite altimetry missions (such as Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich and Jason-3) detect subtle sea-level rises associated with warmer, thermally expanded waters moving across the ocean basin.

## 7. Climate Change and the Future of ENSO

A critical question facing Earth scientists is how anthropogenic climate change intersects with natural ENSO variability. Recent climate model simulations suggest that while the frequency of overall ENSO events may remain stable, the frequency of "super" or extreme El Niño events could increase as greenhouse gas concentrations rise.

A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture (approximately 7% more per degree Celsius of warming), which amplifies the precipitation extremes triggered by El Niño teleconnections—making wet regions dramatically wetter and arid regions substantially drier.
