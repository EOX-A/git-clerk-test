## Sensing the Pulse of Earth's Blue Heart
Covering more than 70% of our planet's surface, the oceans act as Earth's primary climate regulator, absorbing over 90% of excess anthropogenic heat and approximately a quarter of annual carbon emissions. Satellite remote sensing plays an indispensable role in observing these vast aquatic expanses, overcoming the prohibitive logistical barriers of continuous shipboard hydrography.

From polar sun-synchronous orbits, modern constellation missions like [Copernicus Sentinel-3](https://sentinels.copernicus.eu/web/sentinel/missions/sentinel-3) and Sentinel-6 Michael Freilich operate dual suites of optical and microwave sensors. The Ocean and Land Colour Instrument (OLCI) measures spectral radiance variations caused by phytoplankton chlorophyll concentrations, mapping primary productivity across oceanic gyres. Simultaneously, dual-frequency synthetic aperture radar altimeters record sea surface height anomalies with centimeter-scale precision, charting geostrophic ocean currents, eddies, and long-term sea-level rise.

Combining optical radiometry, infrared thermal profiling, and radar altimetry empowers the [Copernicus Marine Service](https://marine.copernicus.eu/) to deliver near-real-time operational forecasts, detect marine heatwaves, and safeguard vulnerable marine biodiversity hotspots.

#### Sargasso Sea & Oligotrophic Central Gyres
Ocean color sensors detect floating pelagic Sargassum aggregations and track the low-nutrient oceanic desert defined by clockwise subtropical circulation ([Copernicus Sentinel-2 & 3](https://browser.dataspace.copernicus.eu/)).

## Sustaining Operational Marine Intelligence
The integration of spaceborne ocean observations with numerical circulation models has transformed operational oceanography. Constellations such as Sentinel-3, Sentinel-6, and Jason altimetry provide continuous real-time inputs into assimilative ocean models, predicting storm surges, mapping rogue waves, and forecasting marine ecosystem shifts under ongoing climate change.
