## Orbital Perspectives on the Global Marine Engine
The world ocean covers more than 70% of Earth's surface and absorbs over 90% of the excess heat generated by anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. Tracking these vast, turbulent waters requires continuous, synoptic Earth observation data from satellite constellations alongside in situ profiling networks like Argo floats.

Key measurement regimes include radar altimetry for sea surface height (SSH) and geostrophic currents, radiometric measurements for sea surface temperature (SST) and sea surface salinity (SSS), and optical imaging for marine chlorophyll and phytoplankton blooms. Satellite monitoring networks such as the [Copernicus Marine Service](https://marine.copernicus.eu/) and the [NASA Ocean Biology Processing Group](https://oceancolor.gsfc.nasa.gov/) provide researchers and coastal managers with operational datasets to forecast marine heatwaves, monitor upwelling, and safeguard coastal habitats.

From the energetic meandering eddies of the Gulf Stream to intense equatorial upwelling belts, satellite sensors provide an indispensable macroscope for understanding ocean-climate interactions.

## Outlook for Operational Oceanography
Next-generation satellite missions such as the Surface Water and Ocean Topography (SWOT) satellite and Copernicus Sentinel-3 and Sentinel-6 constellations are providing unprecedented resolution into sub-mesoscale ocean dynamics. These fine-scale eddies, measuring between 1 and 50 kilometers, are critical conduits for vertical ocean mixing and gas exchange with the atmosphere.

As global ocean warming accelerates, assimilating high-resolution satellite observations into numerical ocean prediction models is essential for tracking biodiversity shifts, coastal storm surges, and the health of coral ecosystems worldwide.
